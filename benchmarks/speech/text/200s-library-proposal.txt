I would like to propose a small change to the way our neighbourhood library welcomes new members. At the moment, the first visit involves several separate conversations. Someone explains the borrowing rules at the desk, another person points out the reading rooms, and a printed leaflet describes the online catalogue. Each part is useful, but it is easy to miss something when the building is busy. A short, optional orientation could bring those pieces together without replacing the personal welcome.

The orientation should begin with what a visitor can do today. Show where to return a book, how to find a reserved item, and where to ask for help. Explain which areas are quiet and which are intended for group work. Avoid beginning with a long list of penalties. People are more likely to remember a rule when they understand the service it protects. For example, returning a reserved book on time helps the next reader plan a visit. That explanation is clearer than presenting a fine without any context.

We could test the idea on two afternoons with volunteers rather than changing the permanent schedule immediately. One volunteer would guide a small group around the ground floor while another remained at the desk. Visitors who prefer not to join a group could take a large print map or ask for a brief individual explanation. We should make it clear that joining the orientation is not a condition of membership. It is simply another way to become comfortable using the building.

The map deserves particular attention. It should use the same names as the signs on the shelves and include the accessible entrance, the lift, and the nearest public transport stop. A beautiful map is not helpful if its labels differ from the words a visitor sees on the wall. Before printing a large batch, ask someone unfamiliar with the building to follow it without assistance. Watch where they hesitate, then revise the confusing parts. That practical check will teach us more than a discussion about whether the design looks finished.

We should also consider visitors who cannot attend in person. A plain text version of the orientation can explain how to search the catalogue, request a delivery, and contact a librarian. It should work on a small screen and should not require an account merely to read the instructions. Translations can follow once the original wording is stable. Keeping the source text short will make both translation and future maintenance easier.

At the end of the trial, we can compare staff observations with a few voluntary comments from visitors. We do not need to collect names or detailed personal information. The questions are straightforward: did people find the service they wanted, did the orientation reduce confusion, and could staff run it without neglecting other duties? If the answers are encouraging, we can keep the useful parts and adjust the rest. The goal is a more welcoming first visit, not a new programme that creates work without helping anyone.
